FlyRank ML Internship · Machine Learning Capstone

Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

Author: A D S ABHISHEK  ·  Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring  ·  Date: August 2026

Abstract. This research asks whether historical content-performance signals can support a repeatable ranking of content items for editorial review and potential refresh. The analysis uses the FlyRank search-intelligence warehouse, with historical observations transformed into safe prior-window features and a future-looking performance-decline outcome. A transparent recent-performance baseline is compared with a machine-learning ranking approach using a time-aware evaluation design and leakage checks. The output is a ranked action queue with reason codes intended to help editors prioritize review rather than automatically change content. Results are framed as observed, measured, directional, and decision-support evidence, not as proof of Google's ranking algorithm or causal evidence that refreshing content changes search performance.

1. Introduction / Problem Statement

The practical decision is which content items should be reviewed first for possible refresh, investigation, monitoring, or protection. The unit of analysis is a content item observed over a defined historical performance window. The output is a ranked refresh-opportunity score that a human editor can use to prioritize limited editorial effort.

The cost of a wrong call is asymmetric: refreshing healthy content can waste effort and introduce unnecessary changes, while failing to review genuinely deteriorating content can allow performance problems to persist. Historical warehouse data makes it possible to combine multiple signals consistently and test whether those signals provide useful ranking information for a future outcome.

2. Data

The capstone moves beyond the earlier prototype and uses the real FlyRank warehouse. The primary daily performance source is fact_content_daily_performance, documented at report_date × client × content grain. Content-level attributes are obtained from dim_content.

The public-safe analysis excludes client names, domains, URLs, private queries, credentials, raw exports, and other client-identifying information. Pseudonymous identifiers may be used internally for grouping, joins, or partitioning, but are not predictive features.

Label-derived and future-derived fields are excluded from the predictive feature set when they encode the outcome or information unavailable at scoring time. Features are constructed only from the historical window; the future window is used only to construct the evaluation outcome.

3. Methodology

Research question: Can prior content-performance signals rank items that are more likely to experience a meaningful future performance decline?
Target: A future-decline indicator defined from subsequent performance relative to the preceding feature window, using a pre-specified meaningful threshold.
Features: Safe historical performance, recent movement, engagement measures where available, historical level, volatility, and eligible content characteristics available before the prediction point.
Baseline: A transparent recent-performance deterioration score.
Validation: Time-aware holdout so future information cannot enter model development. Grouping is applied where needed to prevent inappropriate cross-group leakage.

5. Interpretation

The measured results show that the model ranked the held-out observations more effectively than the transparent baseline on the reported discrimination and top-20 ranking metrics: ROC-AUC increased from 0.858955 to 1.000000, Precision@20 from 0.700000 to 1.000000, and Lift@20 from 2.371021× to 3.387173×. Recall@20 increased from 0.016627 to 0.023753, meaning the top-20 queue captures a larger, but still small, share of the positive cases.

These results support the model as a ranking aid for the evaluated holdout, but the perfect ROC-AUC should be treated cautiously and rechecked for leakage or overly informative feature construction in any further iteration. The results do not establish causality or generalize beyond the evaluated data and validation design.

6. Limitations & Honest Framing

  • The future-decline label is a proxy for an editorial review opportunity, not a direct measurement of whether content truly “needs” a refresh.
  • Historical associations do not establish that a refresh causes recovery or that any observed change is caused by an editorial intervention.
  • The results depend on the available warehouse release, observation windows, target threshold, class balance, and validation design.
  • The analysis does not predict or prove Google's ranking algorithm.
  • Recommendations are decision support for human review, not automatic publishing decisions.

7. Ranked Recommendations

Priority 1 — Review first: content with the strongest model-supported evidence of future decline and clear supporting historical signals.
Priority 2 — Investigate: content with moderate risk or unusual movement where an editor should inspect the underlying signals.
Priority 3 — Monitor: content showing emerging negative movement but insufficient evidence for immediate intervention.
Protect: content with healthy historical behavior and little evidence supporting unnecessary intervention.

Editors should use the ranking as a triage queue, inspect the reason codes and underlying evidence, and make the final refresh or monitoring decision themselves.
